    5-axis machining: 3+2 or simultaneous, kinematics, RTCP and tilted planes

    The difference between positional (3+2) and simultaneous 5-axis, table-table, head-head and head-table kinematics, RTCP (G43.4) and tilted planes (G68.2, CYCLE800), and what it takes to succeed: CAM, post, simulation, fixturing.

    Reviewed November 26, 2026 3 min read Level : Advanced DraftWritten by CoupeFab from the official documents cited. Technical review by Michaël Brassard pending.

    On this page

    A 5-axis machine adds two rotary axes to the three linear axes. But “doing 5-axis” covers two very different practices: positional (3+2) — most parts — and simultaneous 5-axis, essential for freeform surfaces.

    3+2 or simultaneous?

    Criterion3+2 (positional)Simultaneous 5-axis
    PrincipleRotary axes orient then lock; you machine in X/Y/Z in a tilted planeAll five axes move together while cutting
    PartsAngled faces and holes, several faces in one setupBlades, impellers, molds, sculpted surfaces, swarf cutting
    AdvantagesRigidity, short tools, simpler programmingFull access, short tools even in deep cavities, better finish
    Requirements3+2 CAM, tilted plane on the controlMulti-axis CAM, machine simulation, certified post

    Kinematics

    TypeWhat movesFor
    Table-table (trunnion)The part tilts and rotatesSmall and medium parts, very common
    Head-headThe tool tilts and rotates, the part stays putLarge, heavy parts
    Head-tableOne axis in the head, one in the tableCompromise

    RTCP and tilted planes

    • RTCP / TCPC (Fanuc G43.4, equivalents on Heidenhain, Siemens…): the control compensates pivots and tool length so the tool tip follows the programmed path. Essential for simultaneous work; it also makes programs less dependent on the exact part position.
    • Tilted work planes (Fanuc G68.2, Siemens CYCLE800, Heidenhain PLANE): the control defines a tilted frame for 3+2.

    These are control options: check they’re enabled on your machine (see Fanuc, Haas and Siemens Sinumerik).

    What it takes to succeed

    1. Multi-axis CAM with the machine defined (see ESPRIT CAM, Mastercam 2D and other 5-axis modules).
    2. A post certified for the exact kinematics (see Post processors).
    3. Full machine simulation: head/table/fixture collisions, axis limits.
    4. Clearance-friendly fixturing: 5-axis vises, dovetails, raised fixtures (see Fixturing).
    5. Calibration of rotary centres (manufacturer’s probing cycles).

    Checklist before the first part

    • RTCP / tilted plane options enabled
    • Rotary centres calibrated
    • Collision-free simulation with the real fixture and holder
    • First run at reduced speed and above the part
    • Origins (G54…) consistent with CAM (see Work offsets (G54))

    Common mistakes

    MistakeConsequence
    Simulating without the fixture or holderReal collision
    “Generic 5-axis” postUnexpected rotary moves, scrapped part
    Simultaneous where 3+2 would doLonger programming, less rigidity
    Uncalibrated rotary centresPosition errors on angled faces

    FAQ

    Is a 4th axis enough?

    For cylindrical parts or faces around one axis, often yes; 5-axis pays off for compound faces and freeform surfaces.

    Should you start with simultaneous?

    No: most shops first get their return from 3+2 (fewer setups, fewer fixtures).
