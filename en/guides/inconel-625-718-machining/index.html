    Inconel 625 and 718: what they’re for, machining and cutting nickel superalloys

    Why Inconel is so hard to machine (work hardening, heat, abrasion), differences between 625 and 718 (age-hardenable), carbide and ceramic tooling strategy, and waterjet, laser or plasma cutting.

    Reviewed November 26, 2026 3 min read Level : Advanced DraftWritten by CoupeFab from the official documents cited. Technical review by Michaël Brassard pending.

    On this page
    1. 625 vs 718
    2. Machining strategy
    3. Cutting sheet and plate
    4. Checklist
    5. Common mistakes
    6. FAQ

    “Inconel” is a brand (Special Metals) that became the common name for nickel-based superalloys. They keep their strength at high temperature and resist corrosion — exactly what makes them hard to machine: heat stays in the tool, the material work-hardens and it’s abrasive.

    625 vs 718

    CriterionAlloy 625 (UNS N06625)Alloy 718 (UNS N07718)
    HardeningSolid-solution strengthened (no aging)Age-hardenable (precipitation)
    StrengthsCorrosion, weldability, fatigueVery high strength up to high temperature
    Typical usesChemical, marine, cladding, bellowsAerospace (discs, shafts, fasteners), oil and gas
    MachiningDifficultDifficult; even more so after aging

    Machining strategy

    • 718: rough and semi-finish in the solution-annealed condition, age, then finish lightly (Machining Doctor and tooling guides recommend avoiding heavy stock removal in the aged condition).
    • Tools: sharp, positive coated carbide for finishing and for 625; ceramics (round, gradual entry) only for roughing in continuous cuts on a rigid machine, per Sandvik Coromant’s heat-resistant superalloy (HRSA) guide.
    • Minimum chip thickness: never “stroke” the material; too light a feed work-hardens the surface and ruins the next tool.
    • No dwelling in the cut, no re-cutting the work-hardened layer.
    • Coolant in volume, high pressure if possible; very rigid setup.
    • Speeds: well below stainless; follow the tool’s data (see Feeds and speeds) and track wear (see Tool life).

    Cutting sheet and plate

    ProcessNotes
    WaterjetSafest for quality: no heat-affected zone; see Waterjet
    Fiber laserCommon on thin material with nitrogen, depending on machine and recipe
    PlasmaPossible (“stainless/alloy” charts depending on the source), heat zone and edge to clean up
    Saw / abrasive discSlow, high wear

    Checklist

    • Exact alloy and condition (625 annealed; 718 solution-treated or aged, AMS spec)
    • Material certificate and traceability (often required in aerospace)
    • Dedicated tools, new or in good condition; strategy proven on a test
    • Allowance for finishing after aging (718)
    • Price checked: nickel is expensive and volatile (see the price comparison tool)

    Common mistakes

    MistakeConsequence
    Stainless speedsTool destroyed in seconds
    Feed too light “to be safe”Work hardening, accelerated wear
    Ceramics in interrupted cutsInsert chipping
    Roughing 718 that’s already agedCycle time and tooling cost multiplied

    FAQ

    Is Inconel magnetic?

    Alloys 625 and 718 are essentially non-magnetic at room temperature.

    Can Inconel be replaced with 316 stainless?

    Not without the designer’s approval: at high temperature or in very corrosive environments, 316 doesn’t offer the same properties.
