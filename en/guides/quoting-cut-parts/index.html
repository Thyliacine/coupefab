    Quoting laser and plasma cut parts: a complete costing method

    Material with real nesting utilization, cut time (length + pierces), machine hourly rate, consumables, setup, deburring, bending and margin: the step-by-step method with a template worksheet, no made-up numbers.

    Reviewed November 26, 2026 3 min read Level : Intermediate DraftWritten by CoupeFab from the official documents cited. Technical review by Michaël Brassard pending.

    A cut part price isn’t “part weight times the price of steel”. It adds up the material actually consumed, machine time, secondary operations and setup, then a margin. Specialized quoting software uses the same logic; here it is by hand.

    The basic formula

    Cost per part = material + cut time × machine rate + consumables + (setup ÷ quantity) + secondaries

    BlockHow to calculate itWhere the data comes from
    MaterialPart area ÷ nesting utilization × thickness × density × price per weightReal nest (see Nesting); density and published prices in the price comparison tool
    Cut timeCut length ÷ speed + pierce count × pierce time + rapid movesCut chart (see Reading cut charts) or CAM estimate
    Machine rate$/h including depreciation, power, gas, maintenance, operator, overheadYour own figures (see Shop hourly rates (Quebec))
    ConsumablesCost per pierce or per arc-hour (plasma), nozzles and windows (laser)Your wear records
    SetupProgramming, loading the sheet, adjustment, first articleMeasured actual time
    SecondariesDeburring, bending, tapping, welding, paint, packingStandard time per operation

    Shop tip

    A part has one pierce per closed contour (outer profile + each hole). A part with 40 small holes can cost more in pierces than in cut length.

    Step by step

    1. Read the drawing: material, grade, thickness (gauge and decimal, see Sheet metal gauges), quantity, tolerances, finish.
    2. Measure the geometry: total cut length and contour count (CAM or nesting software provides them).
    3. Nest on the real sheet size to get utilization and parts per sheet.
    4. Price material: decide whether scrap is billed (often yes, fully or partly); use a current supplier price.
    5. Price time: chart speed for the thickness, pierce time, allowance for slowdowns (corners, small holes).
    6. Add consumables, setup, secondaries.
    7. Apply margin and sanity-check against the market.
    8. Write assumptions on the quote (material supplied or not, tolerances, edge finish, lead time, material price validity).

    Checklist before sending

    • Material price checked this week (published prices move; see the price comparison tool)
    • Sheet size available from the supplier
    • Deburring and edge requirements included (see Deburring and finishing)
    • Minimum quantity or setup charge stated
    • Quote validity and shipping terms

    Common mistakes

    MistakeEffect
    Material at net part weightScrap unpaid, margin lost
    Ignoring piercesHeavily perforated parts underpriced
    Using the chart’s maximum speedTime underestimated; the machine slows in details
    Forgetting deburringUnbilled labour
    Stale material priceLoss when price rises between quote and purchase

    FAQ

    Should scrap be billed?

    It’s a business decision; many shops bill the full sheet for small runs and a utilization percentage for large ones. Put it in the quote.

    How do I get my hourly rate?

    Divide all annual costs of the machine and its operator by truly billable hours; see Shop hourly rates (Quebec).
