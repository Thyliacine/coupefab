    Materials · stainless

    Stainless steel in the shop: 303, 304 and 316, work hardening, machining, cutting and finish

    303 for machining, 304 for general use, 316 for corrosion: differences, sheet finishes (2B, #4), work hardening and how to avoid it, nitrogen laser and plasma cutting, dedicated tools, linked to published prices.

    Austenitic stainless 303, 304 and 316 cover most shop needs. They look alike, but not when machining or in corrosion. Their big shared trap: work hardening — stainless hardens under the tool if it rubs instead of cutting.

    The three grades

    GradeDistinctive featureUseIn the shop
    303Added sulfur (inclusions that break the chip)Production machined parts (bar)Easiest to machine of the three; lower corrosion resistance and weldability; rare as sheet
    304 / 304LBasic “18-8” stainless; L = low carbon for weldingSheet, tube, food equipment, general useHarder to machine (gummy, work-hardens)
    316 / 316LAdded molybdenum → better chloride resistanceMarine, chemical, pharma, road saltA bit harder still than 304 to machine
    430 (ferritic)Magnetic, no nickelAppliances, decorationCheaper, lower corrosion resistance

    Parts makers quote machinability indexes of about 75–80% for 303, about 45% for 304 and 35–40% for 316 (free-machining steel reference): mostly remember the order 303 > 304 > 316.

    Common sheet finishes

    FinishLookUse
    2BCold-rolled, matte-satinGeneral industrial use
    #4 (brushed)Grained, satinVisible: counters, cladding
    BABright annealedDecorative, reflective

    Order protective film if the face is visible, and specify grain direction for #4 parts.

    Machining without work hardening

    • Sharp positive-rake tools, coated carbide; change them before heavy wear.
    • Firm, continuous feed: never let the tool rub or stop in the cut (no dwell).
    • Cut below the work-hardened layer from the previous pass (enough depth).
    • Rigid setup, generous coolant; moderate speeds per the tool’s data (see Feeds and speeds).

    Cutting stainless

    • Fiber laser with nitrogen: clean, oxide-free edge; a blued edge often means contaminated gas (see Laser: dross and burning).
    • Plasma: stainless charts (air, or specific gases depending on the source, e.g. F5 or H35 on some systems); see Reading cut charts.
    • No standard oxyfuel (see Oxyfuel cutting).

    Avoiding contamination

    • Tools, brushes, discs and tables dedicated to stainless, or protection (film, wood) on steel tables.
    • Embedded iron particles rust and stain: cleaning and passivation per good practice if needed.

    Ordering checklist

    • Exact grade (304 vs 304L, 316 vs 316L)
    • Finish (2B, #4, BA) and protective film
    • Thickness as gauge and decimal (stainless table, see Sheet metal gauges)
    • Mill certificate if required
    • Compare published prices in the price comparison tool

    Common mistakes

    MistakeConsequence
    Choosing 303 for a welded part or salty environmentInadequate weldability and corrosion resistance
    Letting the tool rubWork-hardened surface, burned tool
    Same brushes as for steelContamination rust
    304 in chlorides (salt, pools)Pitting; prefer 316

    FAQ

    Is 304 stainless magnetic?

    Annealed, barely; it can become slightly magnetic after cold work (bending, machining). It isn’t a reliable grade test.

    Is 17-4PH stainless too?

    Yes, a martensitic precipitation-hardening stainless, heat treated: see 17-4PH: conditions A, H900, H1150.
