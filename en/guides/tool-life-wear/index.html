    Cutting tool life: reading wear, extending it and managing it

    Flank wear, crater, built-up edge, chipping, notch, plastic deformation: how to recognize each mode, its cause and fix, the dominant role of cutting speed, and a simple shop tracking system.

    Reviewed November 26, 2026 3 min read Level : Intermediate DraftWritten by CoupeFab from the official documents cited. Technical review by Michaël Brassard pending.

    On this page
    1. Reading wear
    2. What wears most: speed
    3. Improvement approach
    4. Simple tracking system
    5. Checklist
    6. Common mistakes

    8. For Michaël to review
    9. Sources

    A tool always wears; the question is how and how fast. Per Sandvik Coromant, flank wear, steady and predictable, is the desirable mode and often serves as the end-of-life criterion. Every other mode signals a problem to fix.

    Reading wear

    ModeWhat it looks likeTypical causesFix
    Flank wearEven wear band below the edgeNormal wear; too fast if speed is too highReduce speed, more wear-resistant grade
    CraterHollow on the rake faceDiffusion/chemistry at high temperature (high speed)Reduce speed, suitable coated grade
    Built-up edge (BUE)Material welded on the edgeSticky materials (aluminum, stainless, copper), speed/temperature too lowRaise speed, sharper edge, lubrication
    ChippingSmall chips off the edgeInterrupted cuts, vibration, edge too fragileTougher grade, stronger edge prep, rigidity
    Notch wearNotch at depth-of-cut lineWork hardening (stainless, superalloys), scaleVary depth of cut, smaller lead angle
    Plastic deformationSagging edgeExcessive temperature and forceReduce speed/feed, harder grade
    Thermal cracksCracks perpendicular to the edgeThermal shock (intermittent coolant)Continuous coolant or dry per recommendation

    What wears most: speed

    Sandvik Coromant states tool life depends mostly on cutting speed, then feed, and little on depth of cut. Taylor’s equation (V × Tⁿ = C) formalizes the link; useful for reasoning, but limited: newer models add feed and chip thickness.

    In practice: to gain tool life without losing productivity, lower speed a little and raise feed (within tool and finish limits).

    Improvement approach

    1. Look at the worn edge with a loupe (×10) and identify the mode.
    2. Fix the cause (table above), one parameter at a time.
    3. Measure tool life (parts, minutes in cut or length machined).
    4. Standardize: the chosen value goes into the CAM tool library and the setup sheet.

    Simple tracking system

    DataWhy
    Tool reference + gradeCompare suppliers
    Material machinedTool life only means something per material
    Speed, feed, depthReproduce conditions
    Parts or minutes per edgeMain indicator
    End-of-life modeSpot problems (chipping ≠ normal wear)

    Checklist

    • Rigid setup, minimal stick-out
    • Tool runout checked (end mills)
    • Suitable coolant (or air for some operations)
    • Chip evacuation (no re-cutting)
    • Planned tool change before breakage

    Common mistakes

    MistakeConsequence
    Waiting for breakage to change the toolScrapped part, damaged holder
    Reducing feed to “spare” the toolRubbing, work hardening, faster wear
    Ignoring runoutOne flute does all the work
    Changing grade without recording conditionsNo valid comparison

    FAQ

    Should you always use coolant?

    No: some carbide milling operations do better with air or dry (less thermal shock). Follow the tool maker’s recommendation.

    Why does a new end mill break right away?

    Often runout, stick-out, too abrupt an entry or too thick a chip; see Milling troubleshooting.
